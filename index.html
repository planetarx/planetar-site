Finding the ships that go dark.

When a vessel switches off its AIS transponder, it disappears from the usual picture. Planetar learns to recognise that same vessel in satellite radar, harbour cameras, underwater microphones and other sensors, so it can be followed after the beacon goes silent.

Open the live Victoria demo Read the code

How it learns

  1. Watch while the beacon is on

    While a ship broadcasts AIS, its name and position tell us which radar returns, camera frames and sounds belong to it. Every hour of normal traffic gives the model free training examples, with nobody labelling data by hand.

  2. Learn one shared picture

    Each sensor gets its own encoder, trained so that observations of the same vessel land close together whichever sensor made them.

  3. Re-identify when it goes dark

    Once the beacon goes silent, new sightings are matched back to the vessel. Each match has a calibrated confidence and shows the evidence it rests on, and an analyst confirms or rejects it.

Live sites

vic.planetar.ca Live

The analyst workspace on live AIS traffic around Victoria, BC: a channel for each vessel, a map, the entity graph, and a trace of every message from sensor to screen.
orca.planetar.ca In development

Where are the whales around Victoria right now, and why do we think so? It estimates orca positions from how whale-watching boats move in live AIS, and every estimate links to the data behind it.

Open source

The Planetar services are public under the GNU GPL v3. The wire format, zmesg, is Apache-2.0 so anyone can speak it.

  • planetar-broker the message bus, in C
  • planetar-ontology the vessel and entity graph
  • planetar-ui the analyst workspace
  • planetar-ais AIS ingest
  • planetar-sat satellite radar
  • planetar-eo cameras
  • planetar-acoustic hydrophones

About

Planetar is a project of Zax Analytics, an independent Canadian company in Victoria, British Columbia. Steven Ness, the company's founder and CEO, builds it. He brings years of machine-learning work on large unlabelled audio archives, including killer-whale calls from Ocean Networks Canada hydrophones.
